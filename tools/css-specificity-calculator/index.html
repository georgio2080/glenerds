How specificity works

Every selector gets a three-part score: IDs, then classes, attributes, and pseudo-classes, then elements and pseudo-elements. Compare left to right — a single ID outranks any number of classes, and a single class outranks any number of elements. When two rules tie, the one that appears later in your CSS wins. That is the whole game, and most “why won’t this apply” mysteries are just a score you have not counted.

Writing selectors that do not fight you

The practical takeaway: keep scores low and flat. Reach for classes instead of IDs for styling, avoid nesting five levels deep to “be specific,” and treat !important as a fire alarm — it means the scoring got away from you somewhere upstream. When you inherit a stylesheet full of #app #content div.wrapper ul li a, paste the worst offenders into the calculator above and you will see exactly which ones are bullying the rest.

Worked example

#nav .menu li.active scores (1, 2, 1): one ID, two classes, one element. ul li a:hover scores (0, 1, 3): no IDs, one pseudo-class, three elements. In a conflict between the two, the first selector wins — no !important needed, and now you know exactly why.

Frequently asked questions

Does the universal selector count?
No — * adds zero to specificity, as do combinators like > and whitespace. They structure the selector without scoring.
What about !important?
It sits outside the scoring system and beats everything except another !important later in source order (or with higher specificity). Avoid it in favor of better selectors.
How do :not() and :is() score?
:not() adds the specificity of its argument; :is() takes its most specific argument; :where() always scores zero. The calculator handles all three.
Why do frameworks seem to ignore my styles?
Usually because the framework’s selector outscores yours — e.g. two classes beating your one. Paste both selectors above and compare the numbers before reaching for hacks.
