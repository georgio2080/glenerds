How to inspect a used boat

Do it in this order: paperwork first (a boat you cannot legally own is not a deal at any price), then hull and transom, then engine cold-start, then systems and trailer. The tap test — a plastic mallet or screwdriver handle tapped along the hull listening for dull, dead spots — finds delamination no photo will show. Mark each item pass, flag, or fail as you go; the tally converts flags into a rough repair-cost range for the negotiation.

The real price of a used boat

Asking price plus repair exposure equals the boat’s true cost — run that number before falling in love. A $5,000 boat needing $4,000 of engine work is a $9,000 boat with extra steps, and a $9,000 boat in good shape is cheaper. Trailers deserve the same math: a rotten trailer can strand the “deal” in the seller’s driveway. When the true cost approaches a newer boat’s price, buy the newer boat.

Worked example

Flag soft deck spots (typical repair $300–$3,000) and transom flex ($400–$2,500). The tally reads 2 flagged with a rough repair exposure of $700–$5,500 — enough to justify hiring a surveyor, or to make a lower offer with the numbers in hand.

Frequently asked questions

Should I hire a marine surveyor?
For anything beyond a cheap jon boat, yes — lenders and insurers often require it anyway. Use this checklist to screen boats first so you only pay for surveys on real contenders.
What is the most expensive surprise?
Engine rebuilds and transom/hull structural work. Both can exceed the value of an older boat, which is why they sit at the top of the checklist.
What is a tap test?
Tapping the hull and listening: sharp taps mean solid laminate, dull thuds mean delamination or rot underneath. It takes two minutes and finds what eyes miss.
How important is the trailer?
Very. A bad trailer turns every launch into a gamble and costs real money to replace. Inspect it like part of the boat, because it is.
