How special assessments work

When an HOA’s reserves cannot cover a major repair — roof, siding, elevators, paving — the board levies a special assessment: a one-time charge divided among owners. Your share depends on your ownership percentage (often equal per unit, sometimes weighted by unit size). The calculator above takes the project cost, subtracts the reserve funds already saved, and shows your out-of-pocket share plus what it equals in months of regular dues — the number that makes the pain concrete.

Reading the warning signs early

Assessments rarely come from nowhere. Thin reserves relative to the age of big-ticket components is the tell: a 25-year-old roof with 10% of its replacement cost reserved is a future assessment wearing a disguise. Before buying into any HOA, read the reserve study — not just the dues amount — and ask when the last study was done. A study older than five years is itself a red flag.

Worked example

A $240,000 roof project across 40 units with $60,000 in reserves: your equal share is $6,000 gross, minus $1,500 covered by reserves — $4,500 out of pocket, or 12.9 months of $350 dues. Reserves cover only 25% of the project, so the tool raises its thin-reserves warning: this is exactly when large assessments happen.

Frequently asked questions

Can I be assessed right after buying?
Yes — assessments attach to the unit, not the previous owner. This is why HOA due diligence belongs in every condo purchase, not just the home inspection.
Equal split or ownership percentage?
It depends on the HOA’s declaration. Many split equally per unit; some weight by square footage or unit type. Your resale package states the method — use the toggle above to match it.
Can assessments be financed?
Often the HOA offers a payment plan, or you can finance your share independently. Either way, interest makes the true cost higher than the sticker number.
Do reserves really matter that much?
They are the entire ballgame. Well-funded reserves turn a $250,000 roof into a non-event; thin reserves turn it into a five-figure surprise per owner.
