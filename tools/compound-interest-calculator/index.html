Free Compound Interest Calculator

How much will your money actually become? Most compound interest calculators hand you one fantasy number. This one shows the number — then shows what it is really worth: adjusted for inflation into today’s dollars, reduced by fund fees and tax drag, and modeled on contributions that grow with your raises instead of staying flat for 30 years.

Start in Simple mode with four numbers, or flip to Advanced for compounding frequency, one-time deposits, side-by-side scenario comparisons, a reverse goal solver (“I need $1M in 25 years — how much per month?”), and a withdrawal phase that shows how long the money lasts. Everything runs offline in your browser — no account, no email, nothing you type ever leaves your device.

How to use the compound interest calculator

Simple mode needs four numbers: what you are starting with, what you will add each month, how many years you will let it grow, and the average annual return you expect. The result updates instantly — final balance, how much of it you contributed versus how much compounding earned, and a chart of the growth curve with milestone markers.

Advanced mode adds the inputs that separate a toy from a planning tool: compounding frequency, an annual increase to your contributions (so a 3% raise grows your investing too), one-time deposits for windfalls, an expense-ratio fee drag, an annual tax drag, and inflation adjustment into today’s dollars. You can also save up to four scenarios to compare side by side, solve backwards from a target amount, and add a withdrawal phase to see how long the money lasts once you start spending it.

Worked example

$500 a month at an 8% average return for 30 years, starting from zero: you contribute $180,000, compounding adds $565,180, and the final balance is $745,180. Now add a 1% expense ratio — the kind of fee many actively managed funds charge — and the same plan ends at $609,985. The fee quietly costs $135,194, about 18% of the final balance. Tick “show in today’s dollars” at 3% inflation and that $745,180 is roughly $307,000 of buying power. Same plan, three very different numbers — which is exactly why the adjustments exist.

How the math works

No black box. Your effective annual return is your expected return minus the expense-ratio fee drag minus the annual tax drag, floored at zero — fees and taxes are modeled as a straight reduction of the return before compounding starts. That effective return is treated as a nominal APR at your selected compounding frequency: the monthly rate is r = (1 + reff/p)^(p/12) − 1, where p is compounding periods per year. With monthly compounding this is exactly reff/12 — the same convention NerdWallet, Bankrate, and Investor.gov use, so identical inputs give identical answers.

Each simulated month, the balance grows first and then your contribution is added at the end of the month. Yearly contribution increases apply from year two onward; one-time deposits land at the start of their year and start earning immediately. Today’s-dollars mode divides results by (1 + inflation)^t. In the withdrawal phase, withdrawals come out at the start of each month and the remaining balance keeps growing at the same monthly rate.

Frequently asked questions

How do I model monthly contributions that grow with my annual raises?
Switch to Advanced mode and set Annual contribution increase %. A 3% setting raises your monthly contribution 3% every year — the contribution in year y equals your base monthly amount times (1 + increase)^(y−1) — which tracks a real career far better than a flat contribution held constant for 30 years.
What does “show in today’s dollars” actually do?
It divides every result by (1 + inflation)^years so you see buying power instead of a fantasy number. At 3% inflation, $1,000,000 thirty years from now buys what about $412,000 buys today. Nominal dollars impress; today’s dollars plan.
What is the real cost of waiting a few years to start investing?
Save your plan as a scenario, then save a second one with five fewer years and compare them side by side. The gap is usually large, because the earliest contributions compound the longest — starting at 25 instead of 30 is often worth more than doubling your monthly contribution later.
Is a lump sum better than monthly contributions?
Mathematically, money invested earlier wins, so a lump sum you hold today usually beats dribbling it in. In practice most people invest future income, which only arrives monthly. Model both: put the lump sum in “Initial investment” with $0 monthly, save it as a scenario, then flip the inputs and compare.
How much do I need to invest each month to reach $1 million?
Use Advanced mode’s reverse solver: enter $1,000,000 and your time horizon. At an 8% average return with no starting balance, 25 years takes about $1,052 per month — the solver finds it by bisection, testing monthly amounts until the projection lands exactly on your target.
How much do fund fees and taxes really cost me?
They come straight off your return before compounding: effective return = stated return − expense ratio − tax drag. On a $500/month, 30-year plan at 8%, a 1% annual fee alone erases about $135,000 — roughly 18% of the final balance.
